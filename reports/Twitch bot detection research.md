# Separate bot mechanisms to sharpen Headcount

Static code findings below describe baseline commit `2c1792b9f3b0`, before the version 9 implementation. See [implementation coverage](../docs/research-implementation.md) for the resulting changes.

Research date: **October 19, 2026**. Scope: Twitch bot ecosystems, behavior, public-data detection, and the current Headcount calculation. This report synthesizes primary platform documentation, dated research and enforcement records, operator documentation, and static inspection of this checkout. Living documentation reflects what was checked during this research; historical studies describe their original populations and periods. No production replay, contemporary labeled evaluation, or code changes were performed.

**Headcount should rank unexplained activity, while reporting viewer anomalies, automated chat, and follow attacks separately.** The strongest opportunity is to improve evidence quality and control shared causes before adding weights. “Bots” include harmless moderation tools, artificial viewers, fake followers, spam, harassment, and contextual conversational software; each leaves different traces. Public viewer counts and chat-author summaries cannot reveal individual video viewers, their network origins, who commissioned an operation, or the fraction of viewers that are fake. Several existing detectors can respond to one legitimate audience-source change, while account-lookup caching can turn an unresolved response into persistent suspicious evidence. The practical roadmap is to fix those measurement issues, collect event context, establish a reviewable evaluation corpus, and then test cohort and coordination features against hard benign controls. **The current 0–100 quotient has no validated interpretation as probability, bot percentage, or broadcaster culpability.**

## Different bot mechanisms require different conclusions

Video-view inflation is the mechanism most directly relevant to a suspicious audience count. Follow manipulation affects a different counter. Chat automation affects messages and speaking accounts. A service can combine these mechanisms, but their coexistence must be observed rather than assumed. Twitch explicitly distinguishes video viewers from accounts connected to chat: logged-out people can watch, and chat-only bots need not watch video. Headcount observes an even narrower chat population: its `unique_chatters` field counts distinct message authors in each minute. Subtracting known chat bots from video viewers would therefore mix incompatible populations. ([Twitch viewer-count explanation](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/understanding-viewer-count-vs-users-in-chat?language=en_US), [message collector](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:296))

**Utility and community bots** include moderation, command replies, announcements, music requests, games, and engagement tools. Nightbot's own documentation describes these functions; its timers can also depend on recent chat-line activity. Fast responses, repeated text, and regular posting are consequently compatible with healthy community operation. A 2018 observational study found active bots in 63 of 100 sampled Twitch channels, but explicitly did not identify bots intended to masquerade as humans. That result documents useful social automation in its sample, not today's prevalence of fake video viewers. ([Nightbot setup](https://docs.nightbot.tv/setup), [Nightbot API](https://api-docs.nightbot.tv/), [Seering et al., 2018](https://joseph.seering.org/papers/Seering_etal_2018_Social_Chatbots.pdf))

**Broadly present chat automation** needs its own treatment. Analytics or monitoring accounts can appear across many rooms without providing evidence of inflated video consumption. Twitch Insights describes such accounts as probably not watching video, documents a tracking interruption following removal of the TMI chatters endpoint, and had an empty rendered table when retrieved for this research. Its list cannot serve as a complete census or a ground-truth list of viewbots. Twitch also documents several chatbot deployment and authentication paths; badge eligibility depends on those paths. Missing a chatbot badge does not establish human operation. ([Twitch Insights bot list](https://twitchinsights.net/bots), [Twitch Chat & Chatbots](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/chat))

**Follow bots** can create rapid net growth and later disappearance. Twitch's fake-engagement guidance describes batch follows and removals, while also recognizing reciprocal engagement and third-party traffic services. In Headcount, however, the collector fetches follower totals, not individual follow events. A positive difference is net growth between observations; a negative difference is net loss. Neither identifies which accounts moved, whether follows occurred together, or why they disappeared. These are useful event clues, with weaker temporal resolution than the names “burst” and “purge” suggest. ([Twitch fake-engagement guidance](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/how-to-handle-view-follow-bots?language=en_US&sf211146256=1), [follower collection](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/helix/client.go:333))

**Spam and harassment automation** can target a broadcaster rather than benefit them. Twitch's September 2021 complaint alleged repeat account creation, coordinated hate attacks, demonstrations, and shared techniques. Those are allegations in a legal filing, not independent adjudication of every account. Qualitative research also describes hate raids as human–bot coordination, rather than exclusively autonomous software. Detection should describe an attack affecting a channel and retain its context. It should never automatically convert harassment into an accusation that the victim bought engagement. ([Twitch complaint, 2021](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/twitch-hate-raid.pdf), [Cai et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16248))

**Contextual conversational bots** weaken the idea that varied, relevant chat proves a human audience. An author-published Twitch bot already demonstrates EventSub integration, conversation history, configurable prompts, and multiple language-model providers. That artifact establishes accessible capability, not adoption by fraudulent operators. Conversely, repetitive language does not establish automation: commands, chants, copypasta, and reactions to the same event produce repetition among humans. The appropriate research question is whether coordinated behavior and its context discriminate abuse, not whether a sentence sounds artificial. ([Twitch AI bot repository](https://github.com/gabriel-logan/twitch-ai-bot), [Nematzadeh et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837236/))

“Where they come from” has several layers: software authors or hosted services, access to accounts when chatting or following is involved, traffic delivery, and the buyer or instigator. Twitch described commercial sellers and attacks against other broadcasters in 2016; a 2018 injunction required specified services to be disabled. These establish historical business models and enforcement targets. They do not establish today's market size, countries of operation, or the proportions using newly created, aged, purchased, or compromised accounts. Twitch's compromise guidance confirms that account control can change, so account age cannot guarantee continuity of control. This research found no sound basis to claim that most Twitch bots use stolen accounts or any particular proxy infrastructure. ([Twitch artificial-engagement statement, 2016](https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2016/06/17/on-artificial-viewers-followers-and-chat-activity-317906f1c7ea/), [permanent injunction, 2018](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Twitch-bots-ORDER.pdf), [account-compromise guidance](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/account-hacked?language=no))

The scoring implication is to retain separate outcomes for **audience-count anomaly, chat automation, and follow/harassment events**, with authorized automation and unknown explanation explicit. Names, language, posting hours, and overlapping audiences cannot establish an operator's geography, nationality, vendor, or intent. Even a convincing manipulation event does not establish who ordered it.

## Legitimate distribution can trigger several detectors together

Viewer growth without matching chat growth has several plausible causes. Twitch supports video-only embeds with autoplay under documented restrictions. Its front page includes curated and recommended placements. Drops reward time spent watching, supplying a reason to remain without talking. These mechanisms establish alternatives to fake viewers; they do not imply that every embed is benign or that Headcount can identify a stream's referrals. The official front-page and Drops claims here rely on indexed help text where full-page retrieval returned a JavaScript shell. ([Twitch embedding documentation](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/embed/video-and-clips/), [front-page explanation](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/what-is-the-front-page?language=en_US), [Drops explanation](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/mission-based-drops?language=en_US&sfdcIFrameOrigin=null))

This matters causally. A legitimate influx of passive viewers could create an unexplained jump, weak post-spike chat, weak post-spike follows, and low overall chatters per viewer. Four detector outputs would then describe one audience-source change. The inference that they supply four independent reasons for suspicion would be wrong. Headcount should record alternative explanations and represent shared inputs explicitly. Missing evidence of promotion is an unknown context field, not evidence that promotion did not occur.

Chat restrictions further change what a viewer can do. Twitch's verified-chat feature can restrict participation while allowing watching. The current collector records followers-only, slow, subscribers-only, and emote-only state, but score construction reduces restrictions to subscribers/emote modes occupying more than 30% of the stream. Only `chat_engagement` receives that discount. A followers-only interval can suppress newcomer speech precisely when `spike_chat_lift` expects it. **Proposed rule:** evaluate response during each episode using the modes then active; keep mode duration and restriction type separate. This is a mechanism-based correction proposal, not a validated numerical discount. ([Twitch verified chat, 2021](https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2021/09/29/securing-your-chat-with-phone-and-email-verification/), [room state](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:393), [score construction](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/score/score.go:203), [current discount](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/detectors.go:456))

Raids, co-streams, and Shared Chat create legitimate coordination. Headcount already filters Shared Chat messages whose source room differs from the receiving room, which prevents a specific duplication problem. Collaboration membership still matters for interpreting common speakers and simultaneous count changes. Twitch's October 2025 co-streaming announcement also describes combined unique concurrent viewership for approved events; displayed discovery aggregates should not be assumed equivalent to each stream's API count. Exact Helix behavior across these arrangements needs a known-event check before any automatic adjustment. ([Shared Chat documentation](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/chat/irc/#shared-chat), [existing filter](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:302), [co-streaming announcement](https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2025/10/14/co-streaming-on-twitch/))

Some context is feasible now: public chat-settings and Shared Chat-session endpoints accept app tokens. `channel.raid` EventSub needs no broadcaster authorization, although it only reports raids that appear in chat. Other context remains permission-limited: ad schedules require broadcaster authorization, and an arbitrary channel's verified referral or embed breakdown is unavailable through the public inputs reviewed here. Ads belong in annotated evaluation cases, but this research established no universal numerical effect on viewer counts. ([Twitch API reference](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/), [raid subscriptions](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/eventsub/eventsub-subscription-types/#channelraid))

## Published results support coordination research, with strict limits

The closest directly relevant public study is **SHERLOCK**, published in 2019 using Twitch chat collected during August–October 2018. It analyzed 439,000 messages from 690 streams and manually annotated 183 streams, including 24 labeled chatbotted. Its reported end-to-end bot-class precision was **97.0%**, with **94.4% recall**, under its study setup. Features included activity windows, message counts, inter-message delays and their entropy, and subscriber indicators. The finding supports investigating distributions and coordinated groups of speakers. It does not establish accuracy for today's silent viewbotting, validate Headcount's coefficients, or cover current generated conversation. The published repository includes sample logs and historical code; the complete dataset is not simply a ready-to-run public benchmark. ([SHERLOCK author paper](https://nshah.net/publications/SHERLOCK.ASONAM.19.pdf), [author repository](https://github.com/shreya-03/Sherlock))

**FLOCK**, published in 2017, studied coordinated viewing through joint start-time and stay-time patterns. Its May 2016 dataset contained eight hours, 92,044 broadcasts, and over 16 million views. Crucially, it had individual viewing sessions and client IPs, and the platform was undisclosed. Its reported performance belongs to that private-data workload and experimental setup. Headcount cannot reconstruct those individual distributions from aggregate counts or chatter posting spans. Borrowing the coordination hypothesis is reasonable; transplanting its accuracy or claiming the same measurement is not. ([FLOCK author paper](https://nshah.net/publications/FLOCK.WWW.17.pdf))

Research also supplies hard negatives. A study using 2014 Twitch logs associated chat overload with shorter, more repetitive messages and lower per-user output. Its manually inspected sample showed that average inter-message timing alone did not discriminate well, and the authors lacked a holdout for evaluating their bot-filter precision. A later hate-raid study used selected channels, community seed accounts, and message-similarity expansion to define attack accounts. That is useful for building contextual incident labels, but seed-list expansion is not independent proof and its selected sample cannot establish platform prevalence. ([Nematzadeh et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837236/), [Han et al., hate-raid methods](https://kumarde.com/papers/hateraids.pdf))

Cross-platform work reinforces the validation problem. LOBO demonstrated that strong ordinary benchmark performance could fail on unseen bot classes; later research found weak cross-dataset generalization and substantial collection/label artifacts in Twitter benchmarks. These are methodological warnings, not transferable Twitch error rates. Headcount needs tests on unseen channels, future periods, and unfamiliar campaigns rather than random minute splits of familiar streams. ([LOBO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09684), [Hays et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.07015))

Recent commercial publications help identify questions, but cannot supply ground truth. Gamesight's August 2025 analysis compared traffic around a Twitch detection change; Streams Charts' October 2025 FAQ describes suspicious-channel analysis and explicitly distinguishes it from platform-wide fake watch-time share. An enforcement-associated count change is not a verified label for every lost viewer. Proprietary judgments without independent contemporary labels do not justify importing prevalence estimates, weights, or universal chat/view thresholds. **No representative current benchmark using Headcount's exact public inputs was established in this review.** ([Gamesight analysis](https://blog.gamesight.io/is-everyone-viewbotting/), [Streams Charts methodology FAQ](https://streamscharts.com/news/viewbotting-whitepaper-faq))

## The current calculation has specific measurement risks

The checkout uses 12 hand-weighted detectors: viewer shape (`periodic_spikes`, `synchronized_spikes`, `unexplained_jumps`, `start_ramp`); post-spike response (`spike_chat_lift`, `spike_follow_lift`); chat (`chat_engagement`, `chat_repetition`); accounts (`chatter_accounts`, `shared_accounts`); and follows (`follow_bursts`, `follower_purge`). These are sensible investigative categories. The following findings are **verified static code behavior**, while their frequency and impact in production remain unmeasured. ([component assembly](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/score/score.go:231), [weights and families](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/detectors.go:479))

**First, account unavailability is overstated and can persist.** A successful user lookup that omits an ID produces a nil creation date; persistence records `missing=true`. `UnknownUsers` selects IDs without any database row, so a retained missing record is not retried, and a retained found record is not periodically checked for later disappearance. Request errors are retried; this issue concerns omitted IDs in otherwise successful responses. Twitch's `Get Users` response does not provide a suspension-reason field, so “missing” should not mean “suspended bot.” Moreover, `ChatterAccounts` inner-joins cached users, dropping pending IDs from both numerator and denominator. A share can look complete while describing only the resolved subset. ([lookup](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:538), [cache selection and persistence](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/chat.go:108), [account summary](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/score.go:162), [Get Users contract](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/#get-users))

**Second, family caps do not guarantee independent corroboration.** A maximal `periodic_spikes` contributes 0.6 and maximal `spike_chat_lift` contributes 0.5. Their family caps are 0.3 + 0.5 = 0.8 and 0.3 + 0.6 = 0.9, so neither contribution is reduced. The noisy-OR then gives `100 × (1 − (1 − 0.6) × (1 − 0.5)) = 80`. Yet the chat-lift detector depends on the same detected viewer spike. Two families can therefore produce 80 from dependent evidence. This arithmetic does not show that an actual stream is misranked; it disproves the stronger interpretation that different family names ensure causal independence. ([Combine](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/detectors.go:518))

**Third, “confidence” measures availability and span.** It is available detector weight divided by total weight, multiplied by observation span/180 minutes, capped at one. The span comes from the aligned minute-array length, rather than independent count refreshes or fully healthy collection minutes. Some detectors remain available with no discriminating spike event. Rename or explain this as coverage, and report actual healthy minutes, event opportunities, lookup completion, and model validation separately. High coverage can accompany an uncertain explanation. ([combination formula](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/detectors.go:518), [aligned series](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/score/score.go:170))

**Fourth, preprocessing changes event timing.** Repeated viewer values become NaNs and are interpolated between distinct readings. Equality can mean a genuine stable count, not just stale caching. Interpolation can place a change earlier and bridge observation gaps. The scorer's exact “3–5 minutes” Helix-refresh comment was not verified as an official guarantee; Twitch's help supports only a general delay. Preserve raw observations and timestamps, compare held versus interpolated representations, bound gap filling, and test whether suspiciousness survives plausible timing shifts. The synchrony test corrects across partners and discounts broad synchrony, but its binomial null still needs validation against serial dependence, shared events, and repeated rescoring. ([series preparation](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/series.go:15), [synchrony call](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/score/score.go:396), [synchrony test](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/sync.go:55))

**Fifth, collection and baselines need consistent semantics.** `onPrivmsg` increments without visible message-ID deduplication; Twitch documents duplicate and out-of-order delivery. A bounded deduplication cache and source timestamps would prevent that documented transport behavior from becoming apparent chat repetition. The anonymous `justinfan` connection differs from current documented token authentication, which warrants live health testing rather than an assumption of failure. Existing connection/ROOMSTATE-aware handling of quiet minutes is useful and should remain. ([message handler](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:296), [IRC contract](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/chat/irc/#parsing-messages), [connection and health handling](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:151))

Follower interpolation spreads net changes over sample gaps, and anomaly counting can count several interpolated minutes as several bursts or drops. Hot, tracked, and live-cold follower observations use different cadences. Report observed net-change intervals separately from inferred duration. Meanwhile, peer chat baselines use raw joined samples with at least 100 viewers; scoring uses interpolated viewers and accepts at least 50. Peer fitting trims outliers but does not exclude the target channel or balance its repeated streams. Harmonizing definitions and using past-only, target-excluded fits should precede coefficient tuning. ([follower transforms](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/detect/detectors.go:319), [poll cadence](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/tracker/poller.go:239), [peer query](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/score.go:192), [baseline fitting](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/score/baseline.go:65))

Finally, Headcount starts from historical global top-200 entrants and later reduces collection for cold-tier channels. It cannot infer all-Twitch prevalence, and sensitivity in well-observed channels cannot be assumed for channels without chat collection. Smooth inflation, channel-specific operations, older accounts, or contextual generated chat are plausible blind spots inferred from the feature design. Their actual prevalence and evasion success were not measured. ([project scope](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/README.md:1), [chat selection](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/chat/collector.go:206))

## Rank implementation by what can be measured and validated

**Priority 1: repair evidence semantics and expose uncertainty.** Introduce explicit user-lookup states, last-checked time, bounded retries and rechecks, and resolved/all-observed-author coverage. Keep creation date separate from present availability. Add message deduplication and collection-quality fields. Separate viewer, chat, and follow conclusions in the output, and provide “insufficient evidence” for collection failures and unsupported contexts. These are concrete measurement improvements with regression scenarios; they do not require invented weights or additional claims about bot identity.

**Priority 2: create a frozen replay and labeling workflow.** Preserve raw inputs, collection health, contemporaneous context, scorer version, detector outputs, and the evidence available at scoring time. Define labels at stream-episode level: evidence consistent with viewer inflation, automated engagement chat, follow/harassment attack, authorized automation, ordinary activity, and indeterminate. Reviewers must be able to leave invisible viewer composition unresolved. Maintain a probability sample of the actual monitored population, including low scores, plus a separate enriched challenge set. Do not estimate prevalence or calibrate probabilities from an unweighted balanced collection of obvious attacks and clean controls.

Have at least two reviewers independently examine evidence without seeing the quotient, record provenance and episode boundaries, and adjudicate disagreements while retaining indeterminate cases. Include quiet music streams, large raids, co-streams, chat restrictions, collective copypasta, fresh-account community arrivals, Drops, documented promotions, utility bots, and attacks against unwilling broadcasters. Authorized traffic-source evidence and contextual VOD review can improve labels where available. No fixed review count is justified yet: grow the sample until precision uncertainty is acceptable in the deployment population and material subgroups.

**Priority 3: test dependency-aware scoring and event context.** Treat viewer shape and spike response as dependent until ablations demonstrate incremental discrimination. Compare conservative grouping with simple fitted alternatives; do not assume a new formula is calibrated. Add episode-level settings, known raid/collaboration edges, and documented promotions. Replay synchrony against matched nonattack intervals and block/circular shifts that preserve each series' serial structure, gaps, and relevant wall-clock effects. Count false alarms across the full repeated-monitoring pipeline, not only a single partner test.

**Priority 4: study cohorts using retained data before expanding collection.** Current per-stream rows support account-creation cohort concentration, message-count distributions, first/last posting spans, and age-conditioned cross-channel overlap. Compare these with matched peers and the channel's own comparable broadcasts. Use degree-aware comparisons because prolific humans and utility accounts naturally span rooms. First/last posting is not viewer entry/exit or watch duration. A cohort of new accounts is a candidate feature, not proof: promotions and new communities also recruit people together. ([per-stream persistence](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/chat.go:1), [cross-channel query](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/shared.go:21))

**Priority 5: collect compact behavioral summaries only for demonstrated gaps.** Despite the `ChatterMinute` name, persisted chatter records fold into per-stream counts and first/last times; minute-by-minute author occupancy and inter-message gaps cannot be reconstructed. Prospective active-window summaries, gap histograms, burst statistics, command ratios, and authorized-bot metadata could support coordination research. Current minute summaries capture text diversity and top-text counts, but not recurring templates across windows. Derived in-memory similarity counts or sketches could help, with purpose and retention defined; hashes still reveal matches. Raw contextual chat for a small consented review corpus would be a separate collection decision. ([chat storage](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/chat.go:1), [retention](/Users/onnwee/Work/Subcult/sus-quotient/internal/store/retention.go:1))

Viewer IPs, country/ASN, device fingerprints, individual playback sessions and dwell times, authenticated viewer identities, and general referral attribution remain unavailable through this approach. More elaborate arithmetic cannot recover those missing observations. Even moderator-authorized chat lists would identify connected chat accounts, not all video viewers. Features requiring private platform telemetry should remain explicitly out of scope. ([API capabilities](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/))

Validation must keep **channels separate and time moving forward**. Group suspected connected campaigns where possible, keep all snapshots of a stream in one split, and reserve a later prospective test period. Design features and fit past-only baselines on development data; tune on separate validation groups. Test whole detector/family removal, unseen campaigns, languages, categories, and low-coverage cases. Later account disappearance and future peer windows must not leak into an earlier score. Random minute splits would let essentially the same audience and event appear on both sides.

If probability output becomes useful, fit its mapping on a separate representative or appropriately weighted calibration set, then lock thresholds and abstention before final testing. Calibration means predicted frequencies agree with observed labeled outcomes; it is distinct from classification accuracy. Simple logistic and isotonic mappings are candidates to compare when enough labels exist, not prevalidated recipes. An episode-level probability of reviewed chat automation would still say nothing about the percentage of invisible viewers that are fake. ([calibration research](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2016191118), [Guo et al., 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html))

Report precision, recall, precision–recall curves, false alerts per stream and monitored hour, alert persistence, and review workload, broken down by language, category, audience size, and collection quality. For probabilities, add Brier/log loss and reliability assessment with uncertainty. Estimate intervals by resampling channels or campaigns rather than pretending minutes are independent. Precision–recall is especially useful when abuse is rare. Audit fresh random samples and appeals after release, and revalidate after counting, API, collection, or scoring changes. **Validated results currently available here concern source-specific published studies and inspected code behavior; Headcount's real-world accuracy and calibration remain unestablished.** ([precision–recall analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432))

## Source register and evidence limits

**Platform semantics, checked October 19, 2026:** [Twitch API reference](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/), [IRC documentation](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/chat/irc/), [viewer-count help](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/understanding-viewer-count-vs-users-in-chat?language=en_US), and [embed documentation](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/embed/video-and-clips/) are living first-party documents. They define measurement and access contracts, not fraud prevalence. Front-page, Drops, and some fake-engagement help claims used official indexed text when full retrieval returned a shell. No exact Helix refresh guarantee was established.

**Ecosystem and enforcement:** Twitch's [June 17, 2016 statement](https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2016/06/17/on-artificial-viewers-followers-and-chat-activity-317906f1c7ea/), the [January 22, 2018 injunction](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Twitch-bots-ORDER.pdf), and the [September 9, 2021 complaint](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/twitch-hate-raid.pdf) distinguish platform accounts, legal outcomes, and allegations. They establish historical mechanisms rather than current market shares. [Nightbot documentation](https://docs.nightbot.tv/setup) and the [AI bot repository](https://github.com/gabriel-logan/twitch-ai-bot), checked October 2026, establish product capabilities only.

**Empirical foundations:** [SHERLOCK, ASONAM 2019](https://nshah.net/publications/SHERLOCK.ASONAM.19.pdf) uses 2018 Twitch chat and human stream labels; [FLOCK, WWW 2017](https://nshah.net/publications/FLOCK.WWW.17.pdf) uses private 2016 viewing records from an undisclosed platform. [The 2019 overload study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837236/) uses 2014 Twitch logs. None supplies current Headcount validation or a benchmark of invisible viewers under today's public-data constraints.

**Current commercial observations:** [Gamesight, August 29, 2025](https://blog.gamesight.io/is-everyone-viewbotting/) and [Streams Charts' October 23, 2025 FAQ](https://streamscharts.com/news/viewbotting-whitepaper-faq) describe observational/proprietary analyses. Their definitions and denominators differ from Headcount's. The FAQ was available; the underlying whitepaper PDF was not successfully retrieved, so this report makes no claim about unseen methods.

**Validation methods:** [Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432) supports precision–recall analysis; [Dimitriadis, Gneiting and Jordan, 2021](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2016191118) addresses calibration assessment. These inform the proposed evaluation protocol, without establishing detector effectiveness. Local code links identify the inspected implementation; line numbers can drift with later edits. All proposed scoring changes remain hypotheses pending replay and holdout evaluation.

## Conclusion

Headcount's defensible advantage is explainable longitudinal evidence: which counts changed, which speaking cohorts changed with them, what legitimate context was observed, and how reliable those observations were. That supports review and comparison while leaving invisible audience composition and responsibility unresolved. A clearer unknown state will also make later learning more useful, because collection failures and ambiguous events will no longer masquerade as clean negatives or confident positives.

The next implementation slices should be, in order: account-lookup state and coverage; message deduplication and health provenance; replay snapshots and blinded episode labels; dependency-aware combination with episode-level chat/collaboration context; then evaluated cohort features. Only after those slices produce holdout evidence should weights, thresholds, or probability wording change. No new numerical coefficients or claimed detection precision follow from the research alone.
